ARITHMETIC GRAPH AND ADDRESS LEMMA AUDIT
2 November 2026

Scope
-----
This note checks gr:realization, gr:forest, gr:network, gr:programs,
and gr:address in the pinned pointwise 2-converse. It assumes the
coefficient-symbol interface checked in coefficient-trace-audit.txt,
including whole-rational-prime omission, product labels, isolation
and the zero simple--simple contraction. Its arithmetic inputs are
quadratic reciprocity, weak approximation and primes in ray classes.

The conclusion is finite for each chosen binary dimension b. It
does not give one infinite array of primes or a uniform upper bound
on the required prime sizes or coefficient precision.

1. Arithmetic realization with fixed old vertices
-------------------------------------------------
Let F be Q or a quadratic field. Include in the support S primes
whose ideals generate the class group, as well as 2 and the fixed
ramification. The S-squareclass space
  V_S(F)={a in F^times/F^times2: v_q(a) even outside S}
is finite. For each new split prime ideal choose a generator pi_q
whose divisor outside S is q. In the quadratic case its conjugate
generates the other oriented prime. For an inert rational prime p,
the rational generator p has valuation one at its unique prime.

These generators, together with V_S(F), generate every squareclass
allowed to ramify at S and the chosen vertices: divide out the
odd valuations at the new prime ideals, and the remainder belongs
to V_S(F). Thus their quadratic residues record all elementary
Frobenius tests allowed by this support.

To adjoin a split vertex of a prescribed individually realizable
type, start with a template pi_0 at a fresh prime ideal q_0.
Choose a in F^times by weak approximation, close to 1 at S and
with prescribed nonzero residues at all old prime ideals.
Use the ray modulus consisting of those old ideals, sufficiently
deep powers at S, and the required real places. A prime ideal q
in the ray class of (a)q_0 satisfies
  q=(a beta)q_0,       beta=1 at the ray modulus.
Then pi_q=a beta pi_0 has the requested local type and residues.
Primes in this ray class have positive prime-ideal density. Prime
ideals of degree greater than one account for O(sqrt(X)) norms up
to X in a quadratic field, so infinitely many choices have degree
one and hence lie over split rational primes. Finite exclusions
remove only finitely many choices.

At an old inert p, its residue norm F_(p^2)^times -> F_p^times is
surjective, so either rational residue bit can be prescribed.
A new inert vertex instead uses rational congruences and the inert
quadratic class; CRT and Dirichlet give its prime. Choosing vertices
successively realizes all independent forward bits. Hilbert
reciprocity then determines the reverse bits from the fixed support
types. Norm bits are the sum of the two oriented bits plus the
fixed S-unit correction, not additional independent choices.

The same construction replaces a contracted product template by a
fresh prime. It retains the valuations and local squareclasses at S
and the residues at every vertex outside the contracted cluster.
If pi'=a beta product pi_i and a beta is a square unit at S, then
the S-unit in
  Norm(product pi_i)=(product p_i)u,       Norm(pi')=q u
is identical: its valuations and sign are identical. Thus
q/product p_i=Norm(a beta) is a local square at the fixed rational
tests. The squareclass at 2 also preserves the signed-prime product
of p_i^*=(-1/p_i)p_i. No unspecified S-unit is introduced.

A symbol depends only on this graph data. To compare two realizations,
introduce fresh bridge vertices matching the required bits to both,
then replace one vertex at a time. The elementary tests in the
omitted-vertex field coincide at each replacement. A preliminary
bridge handles overlapping realizations.

Primary vertices use generators square at finite S and positive at
real S. Reciprocity makes their fixed radical labels trivial.
They preserve the rational filter. Adding a fixed quadratic field's
ramification to S can force all primary auxiliaries to split there.
Old vertices and old-old incidences remain unchanged.

2. Forest derivatives, including incompatible orientations
----------------------------------------------------------
For a bit z the difference Delta_z f=f(z=0)+f(z=1) is the derivative
of the unique multilinear polynomial of the Boolean function.
Differences commute and Delta_z^2=0.

One edge difference contracts its two endpoints, by the symbol
interface. Induction contracts a tree, multiplying oriented
generators and adding their external bits. Once a root orientation
is chosen, a tree determines every relative orientation uniquely.
A component containing two simple vertices has zero derivative:
its first merger of two simple-containing components is forbidden.

For two differently oriented edges on the same pair, the first
contraction omits one entire rational prime. The second bit is
inertia at its other place, which is also killed in the omitted
source. Hence this length-two cycle gives zero. This uses whole
prime omission, not just omission of one prime ideal.

For a longer cycle, contract a spanning path first. The last edge
then changes only an internal bit of the merged cluster. Its
support type and external product residues are unchanged. If its
alignment differs, the resulting pair is precisely the two-oriented
case just handled. There is no self-residue in the graph data.
Thus every cyclic collection has zero derivative, regardless of
relative orientation. Further differences preserve that zero.

3. A network as an operator on the original terminal polynomial
--------------------------------------------------------------
Fix the original terminal bits as variables, with any baseline
realization. Add a network of primary internal vertices, with no
new direct terminal-terminal edge. All unprogrammed primary
incidences are zero. Expansion in its programmed bits gives
  f(1,...,1)=sum_T (Delta_T f)(0,...,0).
Cyclic T vanish by section 2. A tree containing internal vertices
but no terminal contracts to an isolated primary vertex and also
vanishes. Every surviving component therefore meets a terminal.

In a component meeting t terminals, the primary labels are neutral,
so its contraction has exactly the support and external data of a
contraction of those t original terminals. Its operator degree is
t-1. This identifies the resulting term with an actual derivative
on the original terminal polynomial, rather than merely assigning
a degree to it. Two networks with disjoint internal vertices and
no edges between them compose their operators. Any new cycles in
the combined expansion vanish. Thus total degree greater than the
terminal polynomial's degree annihilates it.

Compatible projections, including the rational norm projection,
commute with these products. A single oriented toggle changes its
rational norm bit once. A projected repeated derivative or a merger
of two simple clusters is zero. Consequently the same network can
act on two differently projected terminal polynomials.

4. The constant and pair coefficients
-------------------------------------
Let Q be the grounded Laplacian on internal vertices, with terminal
incidence columns c_a. Over F_2 its off-diagonal entries are internal
edge bits, and its diagonal entries are total incidence bits.

Write the full Laplacian as DD^t for the graph incidence matrix.
Cauchy--Binet for the internal principal minor shows
  constant operator coefficient = det Q.
The surviving edge sets are exactly forests with one terminal per
component: a cycle gives dependent columns, a component without
a deleted terminal row gives dependent rows, and deleting one row
from a tree gives an invertible incidence matrix by leaf removal.

For the coefficient contracting exactly terminals a and b, apply
Cauchy--Binet with row set I union {a} and column set I union {b}.
Every component has one deleted row-root and one column-root.
This pairs a with b and leaves all other terminals separate. The
minor is
  det [[Q,c_b],[c_a^t,0]]=c_a^t adj(Q)c_b.                         (1)
There is no direct terminal edge, so the corner is zero. Formula
(1) uses the adjugate and is valid even for singular Q.

5. Three-site groups and grounding at every address
--------------------------------------------------
Choose a nonzero target x_* in F_2^b. There is a dual basis
lambda_1,...,lambda_b all taking value 1 at x_*: choose lambda_1
nonzero there and add it to a basis of its annihilator.
The b groups are all active precisely at x_*.

Each group has three primary sites. In its physical coordinates
choose basis e=(1,1,1), u=(1,0,0), v=(0,1,0).
Make the e_i orthonormal and orthogonal to all u_j,v_j; on u,v set
  H(T,B)=[[0,T],[T^t,B]].
Convert this symmetric form to physical coordinates Q. Put h_i=Qe_i.
Each h_i is supported in its own group and is independent of T,B.
Attach h_i to a terminal with activation lambda_i, or to any fixed
terminal in the stationary case.

For any active group subset J,
  sum_(i in J) h_i|J = Q_J 1_J.
Thus programming the off-diagonal entries of Q as internal edges
and these terminal columns gives Q_J as the grounded Laplacian,
including its diagonal, after every address restriction.

To add a column c to two selected terminals with activations
alpha,beta, also add c to a terminal of activation alpha+beta when
these forms are distinct. Its activation is zero at x_* because
both selected terminals are active there. At every address the sum
of these extra columns is zero. When alpha=beta, two columns already
cancel; in the stationary case they cancel as well.
Coincident terminal roles mean addition of columns. Grounding is
therefore preserved in all cases.

6. The singular program
-----------------------
Take T=I+P with P the cyclic permutation matrix on b groups, and
B diagonal with exactly one nonzero entry. On every proper group
subset J, P_J is a union of directed paths and is nilpotent.
Thus T_J and H(T_J,B_J) are invertible, so det Q_J=1.

On the full set, ker T and ker T^t are spanned by 1.
A radical vector of H has v=t1 and T^t u+Bv=0.
Multiplying by 1^t forces t=0 because 1^tB1=1.
Thus Q has a one-dimensional radical generated by r=sum_i u_i.
Over F_2 its adjugate is rr^t: it is symmetric of rank one with
image the radical, and the only nonzero scalar is 1.

Choose c dual to u_1. Then c^t r=1 and h_i^t r=0 for every anchor.
At x_* only the two selected terminal columns pair nontrivially
with r. Formula (1) gives zero constant and exactly the desired
edge derivative in degree one. At other addresses the constant
is 1. In operator notation,
  S_(x_*)=Delta_z+O_(degree>=2),
  S_x=1+O_(degree>=1) for x!=x_*.
For b=1, T=0 and B=1, and this same argument applies.

7. The two nonsingular programs
-------------------------------
Take T=I+L with L the consecutive lower shift and
B_epsilon=epsilon E_bb. Every principal group restriction of T
is invertible. Direct multiplication gives
  H(T,B)^-1=[[T^-t B T^-1,T^-t],[T^-1,0]].
Again use c dual to u_1.

Between epsilon=0 and 1, the change of c^tQ_J^-1c is
  ((T_J^-1)_(b,1))^2,
interpreted as zero when either endpoint is missing.
Since T_J^-1=I+L_J+L_J^2+..., this is 1 exactly when the entire
path of groups is present, hence exactly at x_*.
Anchor pairings do not change because Q_J^-1 h_i=e_i.
At x_* the balancing terminal is absent, so only the selected
pair coefficient changes.

Both programs have constant 1 everywhere. Their difference is
Delta_z plus terms of degree at least two at x_*, and contains
only degree-at-least-two terms elsewhere. These are the exact
constant and first-degree properties needed below; no claim is
made about the higher forest coefficients.

8. Simultaneous success at every nonzero address
------------------------------------------------
For one symbol P_x at address x, choose a top monomial of degree d_x.
For each of its variables adjoin one singular program targeted at x,
using disjoint internal sites. At x these programs spend at least
d_x derivative degrees. Any extra degree, including one from a
program targeted elsewhere, kills P_x. The only surviving term is
the selected top derivative, equal to 1. This works simultaneously
at every nonzero address. A nonzero constant needs no program.

For two nonzero terminal polynomials P_x,R_x, first multiply their
top homogeneous parts in the ordinary polynomial ring over F_2.
That ring is a domain. Choose a monomial M_x with coefficient 1.
Each variable exponent in M_x is 0, 1 or 2, and their sum is
deg P_x+deg R_x.

For exponent two use one singular program. At its target it has
zero constant in both polynomial factors, so it must spend one
degree in each. For exponent one use one nonsingular program with
an independent choice epsilon.

Multiply the final evaluations of both symbols over all nonzero
addresses, and sum this product over all epsilon choices.
A term using only constants from a given nonsingular program
cancels, since those constants do not depend on epsilon. Every
remaining term spends at least one degree for that program.
The sum of all these minimum costs, including the two-degree cost
of each singular program, is exactly
  sum_(x!=0)(deg P_x+deg R_x).
No surviving term can exceed this budget, because then some
polynomial receives more derivatives than its degree.

Hence every singular program spends exactly one degree in each
factor at its own target and zero elsewhere. Every nonsingular
program spends exactly one degree in one factor. After summing
epsilon, its degree-one change is zero away from its target and
is Delta_z at its target. All unknown higher-degree terms vanish.

At a fixed address let Z_2,Z_1 be its exponent-two and exponent-one
variables. The surviving allocation sum is
  sum_(J subset Z_1)
    [z_(Z_2) z_J]P_x^top [z_(Z_2) z_(Z_1 minus J)]R_x^top
  = [M_x](P_x^top R_x^top)=1.
Allocations at distinct addresses are independent, so the full
sum is 1. Some single epsilon choice therefore makes every factor
equal to 1. This proves simultaneous success of both tests.

The ordinary polynomial product is essential. For P_x=R_x=z,
the monomial is z^2 and requires one singular program acting once
on each factor. Reducing z^2 to z before making the allocation
would invalidate the argument.

All new site activations are nonzero linear forms. Every site is
absent at address zero. Primary types preserve the filter and
fixed splitting conditions. The arithmetic realization of section
1 then supplies distinct primes, leaving every original vertex
and old-old incidence unchanged.

9. Verification scope and remaining work
----------------------------------------
The general argument above proves the address lemma from the
stated symbol rules. As an additional finite arithmetic check,
verify-address-networks.js checks physical-coordinate grounding,
the singular ranks and radical, and the nonsingular target
coefficient for b=1,...,7 and all 254 group subsets. All pass.
These finite checks support the matrix transcription; the proof
for arbitrary b is in sections 5--8.

For a moving finite-precision symbol, choose b, terminals and all
networks first. Their weight and the list of tests are then finite.
Choose an actual coefficient stage satisfying those requirements.
The unit valuation constant is the fixed constant from the
coefficient audit, not a bound supplied by this graph construction.

The subsequent nonvanishing-audit.txt and
restricted-pointwise-assembly.txt supply the rough nonvanishing,
seed/reserve and final assembly steps for the restricted non-CM family.
This graph argument alone does not establish the restricted
positive-rank theorem or the requested universal rational equation.
